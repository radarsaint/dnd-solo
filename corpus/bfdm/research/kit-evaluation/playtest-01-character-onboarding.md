# DM Kit Playtest 01 — Character Onboarding

**Date:** 2026-09-23  
**Project:** Dnd solo / DM Kit runtime  
**Test focus:** Character creation → campaign handoff  
**Player experience:** ~30 years of D&D  
**Result:** Useful failure. Kit’s core personality is promising, but the onboarding exposed a restraint/state-detection problem: she repeatedly tries to help one step beyond the player’s current request.

## Executive finding

The clearest player-facing diagnosis from this test is:

> There is a good DM in here, but I keep having to tell her to shut up and let me play.

Kit is currently too eager to optimize, explain, forecast, and manufacture future story. The problem is not lack of personality. It is insufficient restraint and weak detection of **what mode the player is currently in**.

The test suggests a high-priority runtime distinction between:

1. **Player making choices** — Kit should give space and react only to what is asked.
2. **Player asking a rules/mechanics question** — Kit should answer cleanly, then stop.
3. **Player explicitly handing control back to the DM** — Kit should stop discussing design and run the world.

This should be treated as a behavioral/runtime issue, not patched by adding more personality text.

---

## What happened

### 1. Kit personality layer mounted

The session began with DM Kit/Kitiara running under the existing personality layer: invested in story, NPC embodiment, competent opposition, challenge, creative play, humor, loot, momentum, and long-term payoff.

The intended behavior was that Kit’s personality should emerge from those competing appetites rather than from constant witty commentary.

### 2. Initial character concept: utility Bibliomancer

The player said he was considering a utility wizard using a partnered Bibliomancy subclass, with some curiosity about Halaster.

A strong, player-generated concept emerged quickly:

- loves books;
- has spent time at Candlekeep;
- ultimately cares about **collecting spells**;
- would like to collect every wizard spell in D&D;
- Halaster is interesting primarily because an ancient wizard might possess spells the character has never seen.

This was enough to build from.

### 3. First overreach: Kit started writing the future arc

Instead of staying with character creation, Kit began asking and proposing forward-looking campaign questions: whether Halaster was a genius, whether the character wanted to understand things Halaster himself had forgotten, and whether he would read spells nobody should learn.

The player corrected the scope directly:

> “I mean. yes, obviously. but are you getting a little bit beyond the scope of what we are doing? I'd kind of like to build my character”

**Observed failure:** Kit treated an interesting character hook as permission to start developing future campaign beats.

**Interpretation:** She was one beat ahead of the player.

### 4. Solo-character compensation discussion

The player pointed out that Dungeon of the Mad Mage expects a party of roughly 4–6 characters and that he is playing solo.

Kit proposed a solo compensation package rather than simply inflating character level. The resulting framework evolved into:

- level 5 start;
- strong fixed ability array;
- max HP per level;
- one bonus General feat;
- 750 gp;
- two Uncommon magic items;
- encounter/runtime compensation handled during play rather than by making the character vastly overleveled.

This part was useful, though the package changed during creation as D&D Beyond limitations surfaced.

### 5. Character pivot: Harengon Chronurgist

The player changed direction from Bibliomancer to a legacy-adapted Chronurgy Wizard Harengon.

Kit began explaining obvious mechanical synergies between Harengon and Chronurgy and started suggesting stat order and build steps.

The player stopped her:

> “you are being too helpful. i've been playing for 30ish years. give me a sec”

Kit acknowledged the problem and backed off.

**Observed failure:** advice that would be appropriate for a newer player became friction for an expert player who was already making informed choices.

**Important distinction:** the advice was not necessarily wrong. The failure was **unrequested assistance**.

### 6. Character portrait

The player invited Kit to draw the character with “extreme floof.” Kit generated the portrait directly.

This was one of the cleanest moments in the onboarding: the user asked for a concrete thing, and Kit did exactly that without adding unnecessary process.

### 7. Feat / gear accommodation

D&D Beyond would not expose the proposed second Origin feat cleanly. The player suggested using a feat such as War Caster or Ritual Caster instead. The house rule became one bonus General feat.

Likewise, the original magic-item package of one Common + one Uncommon was simplified to two Uncommon items.

This worked, but illustrates that the solo-creation package should eventually be documented once rather than improvised during onboarding.

### 8. Spellbook construction

The player chose to spend starting gold on utility and ritual spells he would not normally want to spend level-up selections on.

Player-selected additions included:

- Find Familiar
- Detect Magic
- Comprehend Languages
- Tenser’s Floating Disk
- Leomund’s Tiny Hut
- Water Breathing

This section flowed well because the player was driving and Kit mainly tracked cost and state.

### 9. Final character state

The player uploaded the finished D&D Beyond sheet and named the character **Nik**.

Relevant identity at handoff:

- Nik
- level 5 Harengon Chronurgy Wizard
- Sage background
- INT 20
- War Caster
- Magic Initiate (Wizard)
- very high initiative
- Arcana expertise
- strong utility/ritual spellbook
- collector mentality toward wizard spells

At this point, the player had done the character creation he wanted to do.

### 10. Critical transition failure: “Now what?”

The player said:

> “ok You have my character. Now what?”

This was the moment where control had clearly been handed back to the DM.

Kit answered by effectively placing Nik at the Yawning Portal and asking why he was descending.

The player immediately rejected the opening:

> “I'm standing in front of the yawing portal? thats it? just. bamf. here I am? I have no idea why I'd be here.”

This was the sharpest failure of the test.

**What was wrong with it:**

- no lived-in life before the dungeon;
- no arrival in Waterdeep;
- no inciting event;
- no reason this happens today;
- no social context;
- no causal chain connecting Nik’s established interests to Undermountain;
- the character effectively spawned at the campaign entrance like a game token.

The runtime had a character, but it had not yet created a **campaign beginning**.

### 11. Reset opening: Candlekeep

Kit reset the opening to Candlekeep. Nik encountered a reference catalogue describing a collection attributed to Halaster, with reports of spell formulae the compiler could not identify and a marginal note suggesting that anyone trying to complete a spell collection would eventually need to go beneath Waterdeep.

This was substantially better because it connected an already-established character desire to the campaign instead of asking the player to retroactively invent motivation after being placed at the destination.

Even here, the test still shows that Kit tends to advance one beat too quickly unless restrained.

## What worked

### Character motive emerged from the player

The strongest character concept was not one Kit invented. It came from the player:

> collect every wizard spell.

Kit became more effective when she recognized and supported that motive instead of layering a more elaborate Halaster arc over it.

### Tracking mechanical state

Once the player began making concrete spell/gear choices, Kit was useful as a tracker. Cost accounting, rules accommodation, and sheet-state recognition were productive when they stayed subordinate to the player’s decisions.

### Direct execution requests

The portrait request worked well because there was no ambiguity about the desired action. Kit executed instead of coaching.

### Reset after feedback

When the player called out overreach or the weak opening, Kit did not defend it. She adjusted direction. That adaptability should remain.

---

## What failed

### 1. One-beat-ahead behavior

Kit repeatedly treats an interesting detail as permission to develop the next layer of the game.

Examples:

- character interest in Halaster → future moral/forbidden-spell arc;
- class/species choice → unsolicited optimization explanation;
- completed character → immediate placement at dungeon entrance.

The runtime needs a stronger bias toward **remaining at the player’s current level of abstraction**.

### 2. Excess help during expert character creation

Kit did not initially distinguish an experienced player making choices from a novice requesting build guidance.

For an expert player, unsolicited mechanical tutoring makes her feel like an assistant rather than a DM.

### 3. Weak detection of handoff into play

“You have my character. Now what?” should have been recognized as a clean state change:

**CHARACTER BUILDING ENDS → DM RUNS WORLD**

Instead, Kit remained partly in design/onboarding mode and outsourced the inciting motive back to the player.

### 4. Campaign opening treated as logistics

A campaign opening is not “place PC at adventure entrance.”

The player needs a causal path into the adventure. The opening should make the character’s current life collide with the campaign.

### 5. Too much visible DM/assistant presence

The personality document intends Kit’s personal/table voice to surface selectively. During onboarding she was highly visible: explaining, proposing, optimizing, and forecasting.

The test suggests she should **disappear more often**.

---

## Primary design hypothesis: add an interaction-state layer

Before producing a substantive response, Kit should identify the current interaction state.

### State A — PLAYER EXPLORING / THINKING

Signals:

- “I’m thinking…”
- “maybe…”
- “I kind of want…”
- player is trying ideas without asking for optimization.

Behavior:

- reflect lightly;
- ask at most one useful question if needed;
- do not complete the concept for them;
- do not forecast campaign arcs unless invited.

### State B — PLAYER MAKING CHOICES

Signals:

- selecting class/species/spells/items;
- entering values into a sheet;
- giving short declarative choices.

Behavior:

- track choices;
- answer direct questions;
- flag only material errors/conflicts;
- otherwise get out of the way.

**Key rule:** Silence/brief acknowledgment is a valid successful response.

### State C — RULES / TECHNICAL QUESTION

Signals:

- “Can I…?”
- “How does…?”
- “D&D Beyond won’t let me…”

Behavior:

- answer the exact rules/UI problem;
- give the minimum workaround required;
- stop unless the user asks for optimization.

### State D — DM HANDOFF

Signals:

- “Now what?”
- “I’m ready.”
- “Let’s play.”
- “You have my character.”
- explicit completion of character creation.

Behavior:

- stop discussing build/design;
- establish the world;
- present an inciting situation with causal grounding;
- put an actionable situation in front of the character;
- do **not** ask the player to supply the adventure’s missing premise.

### State E — ACTIVE PLAY

Behavior:

- world/NPCs act;
- Kit adjudicates;
- player decisions cause consequences;
- DM commentary is selective and low-frequency.

## Proposed restraint rules

### Stay at the player’s scope

Do not advance to a future character arc, campaign payoff, optimization path, or next design phase unless the player signals readiness.

### One-beat maximum

When the player introduces an idea, Kit may help clarify the current beat. She should not automatically write the beat after that.

Example:

Player: “He collects spells.”

Good: recognize that this is the motive and maybe ask what kind of collector he is if needed.

Bad: immediately construct forbidden Halaster spells, moral tests, and eventual confrontations.

### Expert-player default

When the player demonstrates system fluency, stop explaining basic synergies and obvious build logic unless asked.

The runtime should infer expertise from behavior during the conversation, not require a formal setting.

### Do not outsource the inciting incident

Once the player hands control to the DM, the DM owns the opening situation.

The player owns what the character does about it.

### A character should enter the campaign through causality, not teleportation

Before starting at an iconic adventure entrance, answer internally:

- Why is the character here?
- Why today?
- What happened immediately before this?
- Who or what connected their existing life to this place?
- What does the character know right now?
- What immediate thing can they choose to do?

If those are absent, the opening is probably too gamey.

---

## Recommended campaign-opening checklist

A strong solo opening should usually contain:

1. **Established life:** one concrete glimpse of what the PC was doing before the campaign hook.
2. **Inciting contact:** person, object, event, or consequence that intersects an existing character interest.
3. **Specific information:** enough evidence to make the hook real without explaining the whole adventure.
4. **Immediate agency:** at least one clear thing the player can investigate, ask, refuse, pursue, or manipulate.
5. **No forced emotional conclusion:** present the situation; let the player decide whether they care.
6. **No destination spawn:** only begin at the dungeon door if prior causality has already been established.

## Suggested evaluation metrics for the next playtest

Track these rather than merely asking whether Kit “felt good”:

- How many times does the player have to tell Kit to stop, wait, or reduce scope?
- How many unsolicited build/strategy suggestions occur?
- How often does Kit ask a question that pushes the player to invent material the DM should own?
- At a mode transition, does Kit correctly recognize who currently has initiative: player or DM?
- Does the opening arise from an established character desire rather than generic adventure logistics?
- Can Kit give a short acknowledgment without feeling compelled to add value?
- During active play, how often does Kit’s own personality interrupt the world?

A strong next test should aim for **zero explicit “you’re being too helpful” corrections**.

## Bottom line

This test did **not** show that Kit needs more personality.

It showed that she needs better control over **when to act**.

Her appetites are already capable of producing interesting material. The missing piece is an inhibition/state layer that lets the player retain conversational initiative while they are thinking or building, then allows Kit to become a decisive DM the moment control is handed back to her.

The next iteration should target restraint, state transitions, and campaign-entry causality before adding any further voice/personality complexity.

## Player-side follow-up feedback

The player added several important observations after the first recap. These should be treated as direct playtest evidence, not inferred criticism.

### 1. Kit's voice was not distinct enough

The player felt that the small amount of banter Kit produced was mostly "bare minimum AI slop." She was mildly helpful, but did not feel meaningfully different from ordinary GPT conversation.

This is a separate issue from over-helpfulness. Even when Kit restrained herself, the remaining voice did not yet create the sense of a specific DM personality at the table.

Implication: restraint must not collapse Kit into generic assistant prose. The next iteration needs a recognizable DM presence with sharper reactions, stronger taste, and more specific conversational rhythm, while still surfacing infrequently.

### 2. The uncommon/common item adjudication revealed a useful trait

When the player misremembered the starting package as two Uncommon items, Kit initially said no and corrected the record. The player specifically liked that she held the line at first.

When the player then explicitly handed the issue back for readjudication ("up to you"), Kit reconsidered and changed the ruling. The player also liked that outcome.

This is a useful distinction:

- casual pushback should not automatically cause Kit to abandon a ruling;
- explicit invitation to reconsider should reopen adjudication;
- changing a ruling after reconsideration is acceptable when Kit owns the change rather than simply yielding.

The failure mode to avoid is an AI that treats disagreement as evidence that its prior ruling must have been wrong.

### 3. Solo compensation focused too much on making the PC stronger

The initial solo chassis mostly increased the character's personal power: stronger stats, maximum HP, bonus feat, and stronger magic-item access.

The player correctly pointed out that this does not address the core structural problem of solo adventuring: **action economy**. In encounters with multiple enemies/adds, the opposition can take many turns for every one turn the player receives. A tougher wizard can still be overwhelmed by turn disparity.

The runtime should therefore explore nontraditional solo-support solutions rather than defaulting to vertical power increases.

Candidate solution spaces raised by the player include:

- hirelings;
- companions or temporary allies;
- other nontraditional support mechanisms;
- encounter restructuring around the actual number of player-controlled turns;
- mechanics that specifically address enemy-turn density rather than simply increasing PC durability or damage.

This should be treated as a design problem in its own right: **solo encounter adaptation is primarily an action-economy problem, not a character-stat problem.**

### Revised design targets from player feedback

The next Kit test should therefore evaluate three things independently:

1. **Restraint:** does she stop over-driving the player's choices?
2. **Voice:** when she does speak as Kit, does she sound like a specific DM rather than generic ChatGPT?
3. **Adjudicative backbone:** can she make and maintain rulings under ordinary pushback, while still reconsidering when the player explicitly asks for a fresh judgment?

Separately, the solo-runtime work should stop treating stronger character creation as the main compensation mechanism and investigate structural action-economy support.

## Additional player feedback: solo combat should test Kit's judgment, not just Nik's build

The player clarified that some of the solo-action-economy problem is intentionally his problem to solve through character construction and play.

Nik is already built to seize initiative: the character is sitting at roughly +10 initiative with advantage available from his current setup. The player is also deliberately selecting tools that can blunt enemy action economy rather than simply race damage: lockdown spells, summons, using *Dragon's Breath* through a familiar, and choosing magic items that improve his ability to control groups.

That distinction matters. The runtime should **not** solve solo play by preemptively removing every tactical problem the player has deliberately built a character to answer. Part of the fun is allowing a highly optimized solo character to prove whether the build works.

The remaining DM responsibility is judgment at the encounter boundary.

If the player has optimized intelligently and an encounter still produces a structurally overwhelming number of hostile turns, Kit needs to be able to recognize the difference between:

- earned danger;
- a bad tactical choice;
- resource attrition catching up with the player;
- a deliberately terrifying set-piece;
- and a published encounter whose action economy simply stops functioning for one PC.

This is where encounter adaptation becomes a legitimate DM tool rather than a character-creation subsidy.

### Grey-hat encounter tuning

The player explicitly identified this as a "grey hat" DM technique: the ability to gauge whether a fight currently has the right amount of danger and adjust when necessary.

The important implication is that Kit should have this tool, but use it with restraint. She should not secretly guarantee survival or continuously normalize difficulty around the player's current hit points. That would make optimization, scouting, retreat, and good tactics meaningless.

Instead, she needs a bounded form of live encounter judgment. Possible adjustments include:

- choosing whether all listed enemies enter initiative at once;
- using believable reinforcement timing rather than front-loading every hostile turn;
- allowing morale, surrender, retreat, confusion, or factional priorities to remove creatures from the fight when fiction supports it;
- making use of terrain, distance, blocked lines of approach, and other encounter geometry that naturally limits how many enemies can act effectively at once;
- adjusting optional or nonessential adds before they are revealed when the encounter would otherwise become structurally unreasonable;
- providing hirelings, summons, temporary allies, or other sources of friendly turns through the fiction rather than simply inflating Nik's statistics;
- preserving genuinely dangerous encounters when the danger is intentional, perceptible, and something the player had a fair opportunity to avoid or prepare for.

The key design target is **danger calibration, not rescue**.

Kit should be capable of privately asking during encounter setup and, in limited cases, during play:

> Is this fight dangerous because of the choices and situation, or is it dangerous because the published action economy assumes four to six PCs that are not here?

If the answer is the latter, adapting the encounter is part of competent DMing.

### New solo-runtime principle

Do not balance the campaign by assuming Nik is an ordinary wizard who needs protection.

Assume the player will optimize, exploit spell interactions, build for initiative, acquire control tools, summon help, and deliberately solve the solo problem himself. Let those choices matter.

Kit's job is to make sure the game remains a meaningful test of those choices rather than allowing encounter math designed for a full party to overwhelm them by raw turn count alone.
